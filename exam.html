Privacy, Intellectual Property, and Cybercrime

A concise, three-chapter tutorial for Business Law, Computing, and Cybersecurity courses.

Chapter 1 — Privacy & Confidentiality

1.1 What Is Privacy?

Privacy is an individual’s legal right to control how their personal information is collected, used, stored, and disclosed.

Examples of personal information include:

  • Name, address, phone number, identification numbers
  • Medical and health records
  • Financial data and banking details
  • Employment information
  • Online identifiers and browsing or location data

Privacy laws (e.g., the Privacy Act, PIPEDA, provincial privacy statutes) regulate how organizations and public bodies handle this information.

Key Privacy Rights

Individuals typically have rights to:

  • Access their personal information held by an organization
  • Request corrections to inaccurate or incomplete data
  • Know why their data is being collected and how it will be used
  • Give or withdraw consent (subject to legal limits)
  • File complaints with privacy commissioners or regulators

Privacy places obligations on institutions to ensure data is handled fairly, transparently, and securely.

1.2 What Is Confidentiality?

Confidentiality is a duty or obligation to keep certain information secret. It applies to any information shared in confidence, not only personal information.

Examples of information that may be confidential:

  • Client lists and customer data
  • Business strategies and marketing plans
  • Internal processes, formulas, methods
  • Medical, legal, or counselling records
  • Proprietary research and data shared under NDA

Confidentiality duties may arise from:

  • Contracts (e.g., non-disclosure agreements, employment contracts)
  • Professional codes (lawyers, doctors, accountants, therapists)
  • Implied business relationships and fiduciary obligations

Privacy vs. Confidentiality

  • Privacy protects individuals and their personal information.
  • Confidentiality protects information that is shared in trust.

They often overlap but are not the same concept.

1.3 Confidential Information & Trade Secrets

Confidential information is information that is not publicly available and is shared with an expectation that it will be kept private.

Confidential information becomes a trade secret when it meets three conditions:

  • It has real or potential commercial value.
  • It is not publicly known or easily obtainable.
  • It is protected by reasonable measures (e.g., NDAs, restricted access, security policies).

Examples of trade secrets:

  • Formulas and recipes (e.g., a cola recipe)
  • Algorithms and source code
  • Manufacturing processes and methods
  • Strategic customer lists and pricing models
  • Internal “know-how” and workflows

Trade secrets can last indefinitely as long as secrecy is maintained.

1.4 Breach of Confidence (Legal Test)

A breach of confidence occurs when confidential information is misused or disclosed without consent or authority.

Courts generally require proof that:

  1. The information was confidential in nature.
  2. It was communicated in confidence (explicitly or implicitly).
  3. It was misused or disclosed by the person to whom it was communicated, causing or risking harm.

Possible remedies include:

  • Injunctions (to stop further misuse or disclosure)
  • Damages (compensation)
  • Accounting of profits (disgorging profits made through misuse)
Chapter 1 Summary: Privacy is a legal right protecting individuals’ personal information; confidentiality is a duty to keep information shared in trust secret. Trade secrets are valuable confidential business information, protected indefinitely as long as secrecy is preserved, and breaches can lead to legal action for breach of confidence.

Chapter 2 — Intellectual Property (IP)

2.1 What Is Intellectual Property?

Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the mind: inventions, creative works, brand identifiers, confidential business information, and know-how.

Because IP is intangible, it requires special legal protection to prevent others from copying, stealing, or misusing it.

IP rights:

  • Prove ownership of creations
  • Give creators exclusive control and economic benefit
  • Encourage innovation and creativity

The four core categories of IP in this tutorial are:

  • Copyright
  • Trademarks
  • Patents
  • Trade secrets

2.2 Copyright

What Copyright Protects

Copyright protects original expressions, not ideas themselves. Examples:

  • Books, articles, blog posts, and software code
  • Music, films, recordings, choreography
  • Paintings, drawings, photographs, graphic designs
  • Architectural works and digital or web content

Exclusive Rights

The copyright owner has the exclusive right to:

  • Reproduce (copy) the work
  • Publish or distribute it
  • Perform or display it in public
  • Create adaptations or derivative works

Duration

In Canada, copyright typically lasts for the life of the author plus 70 years.

Creation & Ownership

  • Copyright arises automatically when the work is fixed in a tangible form.
  • Employees’ works are usually owned by the employer when created in the course of employment.
  • Creators retain moral rights (e.g., to be named as author, to object to derogatory treatment), even after assigning economic rights.

Fair Dealing

Fair dealing permits limited use of copyrighted works without permission for specific purposes, such as:

  • Research and private study
  • Education
  • Criticism and review
  • News reporting
  • Parody and satire

The use must also be fair (considering purpose, amount, alternatives, etc.).

2.3 Trademarks

What Trademarks Protect

A trademark is a sign that identifies and distinguishes the source of goods or services. It can include:

  • Words, names, and slogans
  • Logos and symbols
  • Designs, shapes, colours, packaging
  • Sounds (and in some cases, other sensory marks)

Trademarks help consumers recognize your brand and prevent competitors from using confusingly similar marks.

Purpose

  • Prevent consumer confusion
  • Protect business reputation and goodwill
  • Differentiate products and services in the marketplace

Rights & Duration

  • Registration grants exclusive nationwide rights to use the trademark.
  • Initial registration period is typically 10 years, renewable indefinitely.
  • Owners can sue for infringement or passing-off when others use confusingly similar marks.

Loss of Trademark Protection

  • Genericide: when a mark becomes the generic name of a product (e.g., “kleenex”).
  • Abandonment: when the trademark is not used for a prolonged period.

2.4 Patents

What Patents Protect

A patent protects new, useful, and inventive inventions. These may include:

  • New products or devices
  • New processes or methods
  • Improvements to existing inventions

Patents protect the functionality of an invention, not its appearance.

Requirements for Patentability

An invention generally must be:

  • Novel – not previously disclosed anywhere in the world
  • Useful – it works and has a practical application
  • Non-obvious – not obvious to a skilled person in the field
  • Fully disclosed – enough detail so others can understand and reproduce it

Patent Rights & Duration

  • A patent grants a 20-year monopoly (from the filing date) to make, use, sell, or import the invention.
  • Patents can be sold or licensed to others for royalties.
  • They help recoup R&D costs and secure competitive advantage.

Not Patentable

  • Abstract ideas, scientific theories, and mathematical formulas
  • Computer programs “as such” (in Canada, subject to specific rules)
  • Purely aesthetic designs (covered by industrial design, not patents)
  • Inventions with illegal or immoral purposes

2.5 Trade Secrets

What Trade Secrets Are

A trade secret is confidential business information that has economic value because it is secret and provides a competitive advantage.

Examples:

  • Recipes and formulas (e.g., a soft drink formula)
  • Proprietary algorithms and models
  • Manufacturing processes and methods
  • Customer lists, pricing strategies, and market intelligence
  • Internal operating procedures and “know-how”

Requirements

To qualify as a trade secret, information must be:

  • Secret – not generally known or easily discoverable
  • Valuable – provides real or potential commercial advantage
  • Protected – subject to reasonable measures such as NDAs, security, and access restrictions

Protection Method & Duration

  • No formal registration; protection is through secrecy and contracts.
  • Enforced through breach-of-confidence or contractual claims.
  • Can last indefinitely, as long as the information remains secret.

2.6 Quick Comparison Chart

IP Type Protects How You Get It Duration Primary Purpose
Copyright Creative expression (texts, code, art, music, etc.) Automatic upon creation Life of author + 70 years Protect artistic and literary works
Trademark Brand identifiers (names, logos, slogans) Use + registration (strongly recommended) 10 years, renewable indefinitely Distinguish goods/services and prevent confusion
Patent New, useful, non-obvious inventions Application and examination 20 years from filing Encourage innovation and protect investment
Trade Secret Confidential, valuable business information Keep it secret + contracts and security Unlimited (while secret) Maintain competitive advantage
Chapter 2 Summary: Copyright protects creative expression; trademarks protect brand identity; patents protect functional inventions; and trade secrets protect valuable confidential business information. Together, these IP regimes transform mental effort into protected, exploitable assets.

Chapter 3 — Cybercrime & Cybersecurity

3.1 What Is Cybercrime?

Cybercrime includes any crime where computers, networks, or digital systems play a central role—either as the target of the attack or as the tool used to commit the offence.

Two Major Categories

  1. Technology-as-Target
    Crimes that directly target systems or data:
    • Hacking and unauthorized access
    • Malware and ransomware attacks
    • Denial-of-service (DoS) and distributed DoS (DDoS)
    • Botnets and large-scale automated attacks
    • Supply-chain attacks and long-term intrusions
  2. Technology-as-Instrument
    Traditional crimes carried out using digital means:
    • Online fraud and phishing schemes
    • Identity theft and credential theft
    • Harassment, cyberbullying, and exploitation
    • Money laundering and trafficking facilitated by digital tools
    • Intellectual property theft and piracy

As technology becomes embedded in everyday life, the scale and impact of cybercrime continue to grow.

3.2 Evolution of Modern Fraud

Fraud has shifted from traditional, in-person scams to highly scalable, online and automated schemes, often aided by AI and social media.

Common modern fraud types include:

  • Cryptocurrency and online investment scams
  • Emergency or “grandparent” scams
  • Job and employment scams (money mule schemes)
  • Tech-support fraud and fake service providers
  • Phishing, smishing (SMS), and vishing (voice phishing)

3.3 Vishing (Voice Phishing)

Vishing uses phone calls or voice messages (sometimes AI-generated) to trick victims into revealing sensitive information or sending money.

Typical vishing techniques:

  • Impersonating banks, government agencies, or tech support
  • Using spoofed caller IDs to appear legitimate
  • Creating urgency (“act now or your account will be frozen”)
  • Exploiting emotions, especially in “emergency” or “grandparent” scams

Red flags include unexpected calls demanding money or data, pressure tactics, and requests for passwords or banking details.

3.4 Common Cyber Threats

Key threats for individuals and organizations:

  • Phishing emails and malicious links
  • Malware, including spyware and ransomware
  • Weak or reused passwords
  • Unsafe use of public Wi-Fi
  • Unpatched or outdated software
  • Identity theft and account takeover
  • Social engineering attacks

Cybersecurity requires both strong technical controls and informed, cautious human behaviour.

3.5 Ransomware & Business Protection

Ransomware is malware that encrypts data or locks systems and demands a ransom (often in cryptocurrency) for decryption.

Prevention Measures

  • Strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication (MFA)
  • Regular software updates and security patches
  • Disabling or limiting risky features like macros in documents
  • Using antivirus, firewalls, and endpoint protection
  • Network segmentation and least-privilege access control
  • Continuous employee training to recognize phishing and scams
  • Routine offline or segregated backups of critical data, tested regularly for restoration
  • Documented incident response plans

If an Attack Happens

  • Immediately disconnect affected systems from the network.
  • Do not pay the ransom impulsively; payment is not guaranteed to work.
  • Contact cybersecurity professionals and relevant authorities.
  • Report the incident to law enforcement and, where required, regulators.
  • Use clean backups to restore systems after the environment is secured.

3.6 Privacy Law & Cybersecurity

Privacy laws require organizations to implement reasonable safeguards to protect personal information. Cybersecurity failures can become privacy violations leading to:

  • Regulatory investigations
  • Fines or penalties (where applicable)
  • Civil lawsuits and class actions
  • Reputational damage and loss of customer trust

Good cybersecurity is therefore both a technical necessity and a legal requirement.

Chapter 3 Summary: Cybercrime uses digital systems both as a direct target and as tools for fraud, theft, and harassment. Modern threats— including phishing, vishing, and ransomware—require strong technical safeguards, well-trained users, and robust incident response. Cybersecurity and privacy compliance are deeply interconnected.
